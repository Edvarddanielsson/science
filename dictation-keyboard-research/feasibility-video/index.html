Private, edit-free Swedish.
Pianissimo and Gemma, with nothing leaving the phone.
Can a fully local Swedish
voice keyboard work on iPhone?
Yes. At least eight apps already
transcribe on the phone.
But iOS makes them all pay three prices.
The keyboard can't use the microphone,
and iOS kills it at around seventy megabytes.
So they all split in two: a thin keyboard,
and the main app,
which records and runs the models in the background.
Price one: the bounce.
To start listening,
the keyboard has to open its app for a moment.
Since iOS 26.4, there's no public way back,
so people swipe back by hand.
So apps keep the session alive for up to thirty minutes.
One bounce, many dictations.
Price two: no graphics chip.
iOS blocks the GPU as soon as
the app leaves the screen.
Open-source Sayboard hard-codes its
language model to the CPU for that reason.
The Neural Engine is the way out,
though Apple's iOS 27 rules for it are unclear.
Price three: memory and time.
Pianissimo plus Gemma needs about 1.3 gigabytes,
held by an app in the background.
On the CPU, Gemma writes about 25 tokens a second,
so polishing a 30-second
note takes around five seconds.
The fix: stream the words, and clean each
sentence while the user is still talking.
How do rivals do it locally?
KeySay runs Whisper,
then Apple's own on-device model for cleanup.
Yaps runs NVIDIA's Parakeet.
Sayboard pairs Whisper or Parakeet
with small Qwen and Gemma models.
Outspoken offers Apple Speech, Parakeet or Whisper,
and rewrites with Apple Intelligence or Qwen.
None of them is built for Swedish.
Parakeet's own model card shows fifteen
percent word errors in Swedish.
Pianissimo reports four and a half on its own test,
and Apple's newest speech tools skip Swedish.
So build the split, and prove four things first.
The session survives heavy apps,
and Pianissimo runs with the screen locked.
Cleanup lands within two seconds,
and memory stays under one and a half gigabytes.
Pass those, and you own private,
edit-free Swedish dictation.
